Reaching out for help can feel vulnerable. By the time someone lands on your website, they are already weighing whether they feel safe enough to take the next step. Trust is not a single feature you add. It is the sum of many small, honest details.

## Sound like a person

Warm, plain language signals that a real, thoughtful human is behind the practice. Clinical jargon and corporate phrasing create distance. Write the way you would speak to someone in a first conversation: clear, kind, and direct.

## Make the about page genuinely human

People want to know who they would be working with. Share your approach and a sense of what working together is like. A real photo and a few specifics do more for trust than a long list of credentials.

## Be specific and honest

Vague claims read as marketing. Specific, accurate statements read as trustworthy. Name who you help, describe how you work, and be upfront about practical details like fees and availability. If you are not sure where details belong, see [What Every Private Practice Website Needs](/free-resources/what-every-private-practice-website-needs.html).

## Make it accessible and fast

A site that loads quickly, works on a phone, and is usable with assistive technology quietly tells visitors you take care with details. Friction and broken pieces do the opposite.

- Readable text size and strong contrast.
- Descriptive links and image alt text.
- No broken links or dead ends.

## Remove small red flags

Little things erode confidence faster than you would expect: outdated copyright dates, placeholder text, mismatched contact details, or a form that does not confirm it was sent. Do a careful pass and fix them.

## Protect privacy and say so

Behavioral health visitors are especially privacy-aware. A clear, honest privacy note and a contact process that does not ask for sensitive details up front both help. Reassure people about what you do and do not collect.
